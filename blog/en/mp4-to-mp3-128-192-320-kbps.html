MP4 to MP3: should you pick 128, 192 or 320 kbps?

5 September 2026FightTech7 min read

When you pull the audio out of a video, the app asks one question before it starts: what quality? Three numbers appear — 128, 192 and 320 kbps — and the reflex is to take the biggest. Sometimes that's right; more often it isn't. Here's what each number means and when you can actually hear the difference.

1. What bitrate actually is

Bitrate is how much data the file spends on each second of audio, measured in kilobits per second (kbps). The higher the number, the more of the original detail the encoder keeps — and the larger the file.

MP3 is a lossy format: it throws away the parts of the sound the ear is least likely to notice, then describes what's left as compactly as it can. Bitrate decides how generous the encoder is allowed to be. At 320 kbps it keeps nearly everything; at 128 kbps it has to discard more, and the first things to go are usually high frequencies — cymbals, breath, the tail of a decaying note.

2. Three settings, three jobs

128 kbps is for speech: podcasts, lectures, interviews, tutorials. The human voice occupies a narrow band, so 128 kbps reproduces it comfortably. An hour of it takes about 55 MB.

192 kbps is the balanced choice and the right answer most of the time. For music heard on ordinary earbuds or a Bluetooth speaker, it's very hard to tell from 320 kbps, while the file is around 40% smaller.

320 kbps is the highest MP3 allows. Choose it when you listen on good headphones, when the source is a high-quality music video, or when you plan to keep the file for years and don't want to regret the setting.

3. How much of the difference you can hear

That depends on three things, in this order of importance: what you listen on, what kind of material it is, and where you are.

Phone speaker128 and 320 are essentially indistinguishable
Ordinary Bluetooth earbuds128 sounds slightly thin up top; 192 and 320 are very close
Good wired headphones, quiet roomWhere the difference is clearest — cymbals and decay tails
In a car or on the streetBackground noise masks most of it

Put another way: if you listen while commuting on Bluetooth earbuds, 192 kbps is a sensible stopping point. If you sit still with a decent pair of headphones, take 320.

4. Quality can't exceed the source

This is the most commonly misunderstood point. Choosing 320 kbps does not make the audio better than the original — it only guarantees the conversion doesn't lose anything further.

If the video's own audio track was encoded at 96 kbps — entirely normal for footage shot on a phone or downloaded from the web — then exporting at 320 kbps just makes a bigger file holding exactly the same information.

So the practical rule is: 192 kbps for almost everything, 320 kbps when you know the source deserves it, 128 kbps when the content is only speech.

5. What it does to file size

A quick way to estimate: each minute of audio takes roughly the bitrate divided by 133, in megabytes.

128 kbps≈ 0.9 MB per minute — 55 MB per hour
192 kbps≈ 1.4 MB per minute — 82 MB per hour
320 kbps≈ 2.4 MB per minute — 137 MB per hour

On a four-minute song, the gap between 192 and 320 is about 4 MB — not worth a moment's thought. Across forty hour-long podcast episodes, it's over 3 GB.

The short version: speech → 128. Not sure → 192. Good music you'll hear on good headphones → 320.

Frequently asked questions

Which should I choose if I'm not sure?

192 kbps. It's the balance point between quality and size, and on most ordinary listening gear you won't distinguish it from 320 kbps.

Does 320 kbps make the audio better than the original?

No. A high bitrate only ensures the conversion loses nothing extra; it cannot restore detail that wasn't in the video to begin with.

How large will the MP3 be?

About 0.9 MB per minute at 128 kbps, 1.4 MB at 192 kbps and 2.4 MB at 320 kbps. A four-minute song at 320 kbps comes out around 9–10 MB.

Is the source video deleted afterwards?

No. The MP3 is created alongside it and the video stays in your library exactly as it was.

Does it need an internet connection?

No. The whole conversion runs on the device, including in airplane mode.

What if I picked the wrong quality?

Just run it again from the same video at a different setting. The original is untouched, so you can redo it as often as you like.
