Convert several videos to MP3 in one go, then tidy the library

5 September 2026FightTech6 min read

Converting one video to MP3 takes seconds. Converting forty — a downloaded course, a stack of conference recordings, or the video folder you've been filling for two years — is a different job: not hard, but it needs a little order if the result is to be a usable library rather than forty near-identical filenames.

1. Preparing before you start

Three things are worth doing first. Each takes under a minute and saves a great deal later.

Delete first. Any video folder contains things you will never listen to again. Removing them before you convert is easier than filtering them out of forty MP3s afterwards.

Check free space. The MP3 sits alongside the video rather than replacing it. Forty hour-long videos at 192 kbps add roughly 3.3 GB.

Plug in. Audio encoding is CPU work. A long run uses noticeable battery, and a charging phone is also less likely to be throttled partway through.

2. One quality setting for the whole set

When you work through a batch, decide the quality once and keep it. A library where some files are 128 and others 320 sounds uneven in loudness and brightness when you listen straight through.

For spoken material — lectures, conferences, interviews — 128 kbps is plenty and keeps the total as small as possible.

For a mix of speech and music, or when you aren't sure, take 192 kbps.

Reserve 320 kbps for a set that is genuinely high-quality music video. On conference recordings it only doubles the size for nothing audible.

3. Cover art keeps the library readable

This is where bulk conversion most often goes wrong. Forty files named VID_20260412_101533 are a technical success and a practical failure.

The app takes a frame from the source video as the audio file's cover art. In a long list the eye catches an image before it reads a word, so you find what you want considerably faster.

The artwork is written into the MP3's own metadata, so it survives into other players and onto a computer.

4. Checking the results afterwards

Once the batch is done, spend two minutes opening each file and playing a few seconds. You're looking for three things:

  • Silent files. Some videos have no audio track at all; the result is a silent file.
  • Recordings that are too quiet. Video shot far from the sound source produces very faint audio, obvious the moment you play it.
  • Duplicates. If you converted the same video twice, this is the moment to drop one.

The source videos are neither changed nor deleted, so anything that came out wrong can simply be converted again.

Once you're satisfied every file is good, you can delete the videos you only wanted the audio from. That's usually the biggest space saving of the whole exercise — the video is many times the size of the audio taken out of it.

The five-step routine

  1. Clear out the video folder, dropping anything you won't listen to.
  2. Choose one quality for the set: 128 for speech, 192 for a mix.
  3. Plug in the charger and work through the videos.
  4. Open the library and play a few seconds of each to catch bad files.
  5. Delete the videos you only needed the audio from.

All of it runs on the device: no network, nothing uploaded, and no watermark on the exported files.

Frequently asked questions

What quality should I use for a set of lectures?

128 kbps. Speech occupies a narrow band, so it's perfectly clear, and across dozens of files the space saving is substantial.

Are the source videos deleted after conversion?

No. The MP3 is written alongside and the video stays in your library. Whether to delete the videos is your decision once you've checked the results.

How much free space do I need?

About 0.9 MB per minute at 128 kbps and 1.4 MB at 192 kbps. Forty hour-long videos at 192 kbps need roughly 3.3 GB.

Where does the cover art come from?

From a frame of the source video, written into the MP3's metadata tags so it still appears in other players.

Is an internet connection needed?

No. The whole process runs on the device, including in airplane mode.

Why plug in the charger for a large batch?

Because audio encoding is CPU-intensive. A long run uses noticeable battery, and a charging phone is also less likely to be throttled partway through.
